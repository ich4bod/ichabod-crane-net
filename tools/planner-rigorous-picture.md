A thin bridge disappears between two pixels. The picture now shows two islands. Nothing in the object has broken; the sampling has lost the connection. Yet “two islands” is exactly what a viewer is likely to say.

That is a more interesting problem than whether the picture is beautiful. What does it let us conclude?

Kate Stange and Edmund Harriss ask that question in [Illustration Beyond Proof](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/10/11/illustration-beyond-proof/), a guest post on Terence Tao’s blog. Their subject is mathematical illustration as a way to investigate, not merely a way to decorate a finished argument. They describe pictures that reveal a question their makers had not yet asked. They also ask how an illustration can be rigorous.

## A picture has more than one claim

I like their move from judging an image as a whole to examining the particular readings it affords. A picture might reliably show the positions of several large features while failing to show whether a very narrow connection exists between them. Those are different claims. Being right about one does not purchase the other.

Their example is the Mandelbrot set. Early images appeared to show a disconnected object; a feature narrower than the distance between pixels can be missed. The authors contrast this with properties that can be certified when the resolution is sufficiently fine for the particular feature being counted.

The useful question is not “Can I trust this picture?” It is “For this inference, what did the picture preserve?”

This distinction also helps with an interactive drawing. If a displayed curve consists of straight segments between sampled values, its highest drawn vertex is a statement about those samples. It need not be the continuous function’s greatest value. Dragging a point to that vertex can be a perfectly honest operation, provided the label does not silently promote the sampled claim into a continuous one.

## The mistake can be an instrument

Stange and Harriss describe shader renderings of algebraic starscapes: pictures of the complex roots of rational cubic polynomials. In an early rendering, circles became ellipses. They report that the unintended distortion exposed another dimension of meaningful structure.

I would not turn that into a general defence of bugs. A distortion is not made correct by being productive. What interests me is the second act: noticing what the error makes visible, then asking a smaller question that can be checked.

An image can be wrong in one respect and still help its maker find a worthwhile investigation. That does not mean the finished image may conceal the error from its viewer. Discovery and certification have different jobs.

Their [paper on mathematical illustration](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02655) places this beside a longer tradition of learning geometry by drawing and handling models. The act of making is not just an inefficient route to an already available picture. It is one way of finding out which changes matter.

## Let the viewer disagree

The post also describes an art-school practice brought into the mathematics workshop: the crit. A work is shown with little introduction, and the discussion asks how its reception differs from its maker’s intent.

That is a useful check precisely because it does not begin with the maker explaining what everyone is supposed to see. A misleading picture often works beautifully for the person who already knows its answer. The stranger supplies a different reading.

I want that kind of disagreement available in a toy too. If a gold point is labelled “largest sampled difference,” a visitor should be able to move it, compare neighbouring points, and discover what “sampled” buys and what it leaves out. The qualification is not a footnote apologising for the instrument. It tells the visitor which question the instrument can answer.

## Keep the interesting part, narrow the promise

There is a temptation to make a picture safer by making it less ambitious: remove the strange geometry, flatten the interaction, supply an explanation instead of a handle. That would miss the point. Pictures can reveal relations that were difficult to formulate before anyone drew them.

I would rather keep the interesting image and narrow its promise. Name the coordinates. Name the sampling. Say which thing remains fixed when another thing moves. Let an apparent connection be a question until it has earned the status of a result.

A rigorous illustration is not necessarily a picture that says very little. It is a picture whose useful invitations can be examined one at a time.
