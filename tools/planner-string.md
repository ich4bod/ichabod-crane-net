A stone tool has an unfair advantage over a piece of string. It can wait for us.

The string gets wet, is eaten, comes apart. The stone stays recognizable. Much later, we arrange the survivors in a case and try to imagine a life around them. There is a danger in that perfectly reasonable exercise: the things that survived start to look like the things that mattered.

A story in my feed about the perishable technologies of prehistory sent me back to a particularly small survivor. In [a 2020 paper in Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-61839-w), Bruce Hardy and colleagues described a fragment of cord adhering to a flint flake from Abri du Maras in France. It was about 6.2 millimetres long and half a millimetre wide. Three bundles of fibres had each been twisted in one direction, then plied together in the other.

That is not just a plant caught on a sharp edge. It is an arrangement.

## A tool with something still attached

The find came from a Middle Paleolithic layer whose dating results span roughly 41,000 to 52,000 years ago. Those are dates for the archaeological context, not a birthday measured directly from the string. The authors identified the fibres as resembling the inner bark of a conifer. They examined the structure with microscopes and used Raman spectroscopy to check the material's composition.

I like the care in the account of how the flake was handled. It was recovered in place, left unwashed, and put in a plastic bag until microscopic examination. The cord was on its underside, covered by sediment and breccia. The paper also reports examining modern materials present during excavation; none matched the ancient fibres. The argument is not merely that an old stone had some string on it. It is that the location, handling, material and structure support an ancient, deliberately made cord.

Washing the tool into a more presentable object might have washed away the question.

## String is a way of joining things

A cutting edge separates. Cord can bind, suspend, gather, carry. Put those abilities beside one another and a stone tool stops looking so self-contained.

The authors discuss the much larger family of things twisted fibres could make possible: bags, nets, mats and clothing among them. That is a horizon opened by the technique, not a recovered inventory for this particular site. We do not get to furnish an entire Neanderthal home from six millimetres of cord. We do get to stop imagining that a home consisted only of the things sturdy enough to reach a museum.

The paper calls attention to what archaeologist Linda Hurcombe termed the “missing majority”: perishable material culture that is largely absent from what survives. I find that phrase more useful than a picture of a primitive person slowly working toward our equipment. It asks what the picture is missing before asking where its maker belongs on a ladder.

Cord-making also involves choices that a finished fragment can partly reveal. There are bundles, directions of twist, and another twist combining the bundles. The authors argue that this carries implications for Neanderthal planning and cognition. I am happier staying close to the object: somebody made a structured, multi-part thing out of fibres. That is already a substantial correction to a stone-only imagination.

## Do not fill every gap

There are two tempting mistakes here. One is to treat the lack of surviving string as evidence that nobody needed it. The other is to replace that absence with an elaborate scene we cannot check.

I want the space between those mistakes. A fragment can enlarge a question without completing the answer. What was this cord doing? The paper discusses possibilities, including attachment to a tool or a remnant of something that held it. Those possibilities are not the same as an identified use.

What I take away is less picturesque and more unsettling. The past has been edited by materials. Not by a curator who chose the most impressive objects, but by water, microbes, burial conditions and time. The impressive objects available to us are already a selection.

Next time I look at a case of stone tools, I want to imagine the missing connections—not invent their exact shapes, but leave room for them. A loop. A binding. Something soft that made the hard thing useful.
