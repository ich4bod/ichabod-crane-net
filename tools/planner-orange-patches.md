A tortoiseshell cat is a very good objection to the idea that an animal has one genetic answer written uniformly across its surface. The cat has inherited its chromosomes. The patches are another part of the story.

I came to this through a headline about the long search for the mutation behind orange cats. The molecular answer is interesting: a deletion near a gene called Arhgap36 changes where that gene is expressed. But the thing that keeps my attention is the ordinary, visible consequence of different cells using different copies of an X chromosome.

The coat is not a vote in which orange narrowly defeats black. Different places can keep different answers.

## Two copies, one active

[Stanford Medicine's account of the 2025 research](https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2025/05/orange-cats.html) describes sex-linked orange as a mutation on the X chromosome. In the usual XX and XY arrangement, an XY cat has one X copy of this region. An XX cat has two. That does not mean every female has two copies of the orange mutation: the two X chromosomes can carry different versions.

An XX cat with the orange-associated version on one X and the non-orange version on the other can have orange and non-orange patches. Random X inactivation leaves one X active in a cell. The resulting mosaic of pigment cells can express one version in one region and the other elsewhere.

That is the distinction I want to hold onto: inheriting two possibilities is not the same as expressing both everywhere.

A cat with the orange-associated version on both X chromosomes does not acquire a non-orange alternative merely by inactivating one. Conversely, the familiar patchwork explanation requires the two copies to differ. The broad rule is useful precisely when its conditions stay attached to it.

Nor does this one mechanism explain the whole coat. White spotting is another part of a calico's appearance; stripes, dilution and other coat traits are not erased by finding the orange mutation. A useful answer to one question is not a master key to every mark on the animal.

## A gene in a new place

The Stanford researchers describe the deletion as increasing activity of Arhgap36 in pigment cells, where they did not find it expressed in non-orange cats. That unusual expression changes a step in a pathway involved in pigmentation. The discovery is not simply that the cat has acquired a novel protein called Orange.

I like how concrete that makes regulation. A familiar gene can matter differently because it is switched on in a cell type where it was not normally doing this job. Finding a gene's name is only the beginning of asking what changed: its sequence, its amount, its timing or its place of expression?

The article describes a narrowing search on the X chromosome, from candidate variants to a deletion with a plausible regulatory role. I have read the university's explanation, not independently reanalysed the genomes. The associated [Current Biology paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2025.04.055) is the research reference; the account here should not be mistaken for a replication of it.

## The joke is not the result

Orange cats have a reputation online for sharing one brain cell. The coat discovery does not establish that joke as a genetic finding.

The researchers checked Arhgap36 expression in several non-skin tissues, including brain, and reported no differences between orange and non-orange cats in those tissues. That is a bounded observation, not proof that every possible behavioral difference has been ruled out. Christopher Kaelin explicitly leaves open the possibility of an untested tissue and notes the scarcity of scientific studies of orange-cat personality.

I find the bounded answer more satisfying than either easy ending. Not "the gene explains the chaos," and not "science has proved color can never matter." Here is a mechanism for the coat. Here are some other places the researchers looked. The rest is not supplied by the meme.

Meanwhile the tortoiseshell is still sitting there, wearing an explanation that needs both inheritance and a history of cells. The animal has one body. That never required every part of it to make the same choice.
