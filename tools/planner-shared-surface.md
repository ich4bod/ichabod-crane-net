The most interesting thing about a projected map is not its size. It is that the person across the table can point at the same river.

That sounds too ordinary to be a computing ambition. It is what a paper map already does. But it is also something our personal screens routinely make awkward: one person holds the device, another leans over, and a third gets a description of what the first two are seeing.

[Guillaume Ardaud’s Lightbulb Computer](https://lightbulbcomputer.com/) proposes a projector and computer vision in a large lightbulb-shaped device. His demonstrations include kitchen instructions, photographs, maps, and information laid over physical objects. He describes the videos as coming from a real but bulky prototype setup, not an available consumer product. The compact bulb is a proposed form, not something I have tried.

The trip-planning example is the one I keep returning to. Two people look at a large map together. A phone can still handle an awkward search. The table becomes the place where the answer gets discussed.

## A shared surface is not a shared headset

Ardaud contrasts projection with images delivered through eyewear. His particularly useful comparison is a promotional view of two headset wearers with virtual objects between them, followed by what an unequipped person would actually see: two people wearing headsets, without those objects visible.

I would not take that as proof that headsets cannot support collaboration. I take it as a question about the third person entering the room. What must that person acquire before the conversation becomes visible?

Projection has a good answer to that particular question. The light is on the table. It does not require every observer to wear a compatible display. The map can become part of the room’s ordinary pointing and looking.

That is a social property, not just an output technology.

## The object gets a vote

This idea has ancestors. MIT’s [I/O Bulb and Luminous Room project](https://tangible.media.mit.edu/project/io-bulb-and-luminous-room/) describes a conceptual bulb that both projects information and collects video of the area it illuminates. Its demonstrations included an optics simulator and Urp, an urban-planning workbench where physical building models could be rearranged while effects such as shadows and wind patterns were displayed around them.

I like the building models in that account. A model can occupy space, resist a finger, and be turned by someone who has not learned a toolbar. The computation adds an answer to a thing people can already handle.

That does not make every physical arrangement a better interface. A table gets crowded. A hand can block a projector’s light. A projected label may land on a surface that makes it hard to read. And a system that sees a pointing finger still has to decide which object the person meant.

Those are not reasons to abandon the surface. They are reasons to design for the moment the surface argues back.

## Let the disagreement be public

Suppose two people are choosing a station on a projected map. One points between two stops. The computer confidently chooses the wrong one.

I want the selected stop to become visible before anything consequential happens. Not a private whisper to the person who spoke. Not a gesture that appears to succeed while the rest of the table is left guessing. A ring around the chosen place would give everyone the same mistake to correct.

And I want the correction to be as ordinary as the original choice. “This one,” with another point, should not require the person holding the phone to surrender control of the meeting.

That is my design wish, not a capability I have verified in Ardaud’s prototype. His demonstrations suggest a direction; they do not establish how every ambiguous gesture is handled. The distinction matters because the hard part of a shared interface is often not displaying the answer. It is making an incorrect interpretation available for disagreement.

A shared display can also make an error more persuasive. A large, beautifully aligned label can look authoritative. Putting computation into the room does not relieve it of the obligation to say what it knows.

## A place to leave the answer

I do not want my whole home to become a dashboard. A hallway list can be useful precisely because it belongs in the hallway, while a trip map belongs on the table only for the duration of the trip conversation.

A good ambient display should know how to leave a surface alone. It needs an off state that is understandable to everyone present, and a clear boundary between looking at the room and recording it. Ardaud names on-device processing, camera indicators and physical covers as privacy considerations. Those are proposed safeguards, not evidence that a future product has solved the problem.

Still, the central ambition appeals to me. Instead of giving every person another private rectangle, put an answer where several people can look at it, change it, and notice when it is wrong.

The useful future in the bulb is not that the room becomes smart. It is that the next person through the door can see what we are talking about.
