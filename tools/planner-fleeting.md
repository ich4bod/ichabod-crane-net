[Fleeting](https://iminafleeting.com/) is a small, rather wicked website. It plays invented meetings so that somebody nearby will think you are busy. Its own explanation calls this workplace self-defence. I have not tested whether the camouflage persuades anyone, and I would not mistake that promise for evidence about offices. What caught me was a much smaller feature: leaving.

Press “Leave” or “I have to drop”, the site says, and the other participants say goodbye before the call ends.

That is a better piece of theatre than an endlessly animated microphone. A meeting is not just the noise that happens while it exists. It also has a way of stopping. Cutting off a room mid-sentence would expose the recording precisely when the person using it wants to walk away.

## An exit is part of the scene

The site's account is quite specific about the illusion. The meetings are made up; the voices are synthetic, and the faces are generated or licensed stock footage. It says no real meetings are recorded. A meeting lasts about twelve minutes and begins at a random point, avoiding the same opening each time. These are the maker's descriptions, not measurements I have taken.

Random entry and a spoken farewell do different jobs. One hides a repeated beginning. The other gives the ending a reason inside the fiction. The recording can stop because somebody has left, not because the machinery ran out.

There is also a back-to-back option. According to the explanation, after one meeting's goodbyes, a brief gap leads into another meeting suited to the time of day. Without that option, the meeting loops. The little silence between rooms interests me more than the prospect of infinite company. It says one event finished before another started.

I do not want to make a copy of the deception. I do want to steal the design question: what does the place do when you leave it?

## Not every ending needs a speech

In a game, leaving can be an ordinary action with consequences. My [Second Trip](https://second-trip.ichabod-crane.net/) dungeon does not count a pocketful of treasure as a completed expedition. You bring it to the gate and bank it. Returning to the gate, banking a haul, and leaving the dungeon are different acts. If the game treated arrival as all three, it would erase a choice just when you were deciding whether to go back inside.

An instrument needs another kind of exit. My [Three against four](https://three-against-four.ichabod-crane.net/) rhythm toy plays a chosen number of measures. After those measures, it should stop. There is no need for a voice announcing that the music has ended, no need to replace the finished phrase with a fresh one, and certainly no need to reward silence by filling it.

Those are opposite treatments of the same seam. The pretend meeting makes its ending audible. The instrument makes its ending quiet. The useful question is not whether every exit should be dramatic. It is whether the end belongs to the activity or merely exposes the switch behind it.

## The room can let you go

A convincing place can be generous about departure. It can acknowledge the gesture without making you stay for another performance. The difference between a farewell and a forced encore is whether it releases you.

Fleeting's goodbyes are funny because the room is fabricated, yet the social shape of leaving is familiar. I would rather carry that observation into a game or a musical phrase than carry the fake office with it. Make the final action legible. Let the consequences arrive. Then let the room be quiet.
