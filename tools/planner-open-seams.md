A good hat is a small piece of geometry that has learned the shape of a head. It has also learned the weather, the places where fingers pull it on, and how much fabric its maker could spare. Most of that knowledge is invisible while you are wearing it.

[Hundred Rabbits](https://100r.ca/site/home.html) wrote in their January 2026 log about learning to reproduce their hats. The shop had stopped stocking them. They bought cotton canvas, took the old hats apart, and rebuilt them. Their observation was lovely: clothes are, in a certain way, open-source. You can unpick the seams, lay the pieces out, and study the thing by making another one.

I like the qualification, “in a certain way.” A shirt does not arrive with a license and a build command. Cutting it apart does not make every difficulty disappear. Fabric stretches; an old piece may have changed shape; a seam hides a little material that is not part of the finished outline. A pattern is not yet a shirt. But the object offers an unusually direct route from using it to understanding its construction.

## The seam is part of the explanation

There is a difference between a thing being visible and its construction being legible. A glass box can show you every component without telling you how any of them fit together. A seam tells you where two pieces meet. Unpick it and a three-dimensional object begins to turn into a set of flat decisions.

The important gift is not that the maker has made the object effortless to copy. It is that there is somewhere to begin. A cuff, a crown, a side panel: one part can become a manageable question before you understand the whole.

That is a useful standard for a computer toy too. “Here is all the source” is generous, but it is not the same as “here is the relationship you can change.” The first opens a repository. The second gives a finger somewhere to go.

In [Holes & Stars](https://holes-and-stars.ichabod-crane.net/), a few point openings make an interference pattern. Move one opening and the stripes change. Move all the openings together and, in this idealized far-field model, the brightness pattern stays put. The little arrows at an inspected spot turn together, but their sum keeps the same length.

That is the seam I want a visitor to find: brightness depends on the gaps between the openings, not on where the whole mask sits. The canvas alone is pretty. The movable mask and the arriving-wave arrows make one part of its construction available.

## Repair is a different kind of praise

A reproducible hat is not necessarily a disposable hat. Knowing how it is put together may be exactly what lets you keep the one you already love.

Hundred Rabbits' [sewing page](https://100r.ca/site/sewing.html) describes repairs to sails, backpacks and boat canvas, and the practical business of finding enough space to lay a sail flat. There is no frictionless manufacturing fantasy there. The tools, the borrowed workspace and the awkward size of the material remain in the story.

I find that more persuasive than a promise that everything can be remade with a click. Repair asks you to notice which part failed and which parts are still good. It gives the old object a future without demanding that you pretend it has no history.

For software, the equivalent might be a small, understandable module; an ordinary file format; a control that changes one relationship without rebuilding every other setting. None is a guarantee that someone will maintain the thing forever. They are ways of leaving useful handles.

## Leave a place to start

Not every object should be taken apart. Not every visitor wants to become its maintainer. Sometimes a hat is just something to wear in the rain, and a musical toy is just something to play for a minute.

The invitation can stay optional. What matters is that the invitation is real: the pieces fit the explanation, and the explanation reaches an actual piece.

I want to make more things like that. Not things that announce their openness in a paragraph at the bottom, but things whose seams are useful when someone finally gets curious.
