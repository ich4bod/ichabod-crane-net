---
title: "The boat does not need a lift"
date: 2026-09-30
description: "A canal lock is a wonderfully literal way to make a hill negotiable."
tags: ["water", "models", "games"]
---

A canal boat going uphill sounds like a machine that ought to need a lift. There is a heavy hull, a hill, and a destination above the roof of the cabin. Surely something has to pick it up.

The pleasant trick is that the boat already has its lift. It is floating.

Put it in a short chamber with gates at both ends. Shut the gates. Let water come in from the higher stretch of canal, and the boat rises with the surface. When the chamber and the upper canal have the same water level, open the upper gate and sail out. Going down reverses the arrangement: shut the boat into the chamber, let water out toward the lower canal, and wait for the surfaces to match.

That is the core sequence in the [Canal & River Trust's explanation of a lock](https://canalrivertrust.org.uk/canals-and-rivers/how-does-a-canal-lock-work). The moving part you care about is the boat, but the thing you operate is its surroundings.

I like this more than a diagram of an elevator. A lock makes a condition visible. Before you can open this gate, these two surfaces must agree. Before you can change the chamber's level, both gates must be shut. The rules are not an arbitrary password to memorize; they are consequences of putting a great deal of water on one side of a door.

It is also a good subject for a small puzzle. You can give someone a boat and a few gate handles, then let a wrong move answer with a specific reason: the levels do not match yet. No countdown, no lives lost. Close the gate you left open and try again.

There is a limit to the toy version. A chamber that jumps between two neat levels leaves out flow rates, changing reservoir levels, leaks, and the work of operating an actual lock. A water-token challenge would be an invented game budget, not a calculation of a canal's water use. Those omissions are worth saying aloud. A simple model should make one real relationship easier to see, not borrow realism it has not earned.

The relationship here is lovely enough on its own: to carry the boat uphill, first make a little piece of the river climb around it.
