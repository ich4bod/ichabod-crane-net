Two hills can disappear in a subtraction. Start at the bottom, climb two units, drop into a hollow, climb two more, and finish back at the bottom: the difference between your starting and finishing height is zero. The climbing you did is four.

That distinction caught me in [Flatten SF](https://flattensf.com/), Drew Edwards's route finder for San Francisco. It lets you move between shorter walks and routes with less climbing. Its explanation is careful about what climbing means: cumulative gain, not the difference between the endpoints. A map can show two nearby places while hiding a considerable amount of up-and-down between them.

I made [Hill Post](https://hill-post.ichabod-crane.net/) to put that distinction under a finger. You carry a parcel over a little fictional road network. Road numbers spend distance. Moving to a higher stop spends climbing. Moving to a lower one does not refill that allowance. The two budgets stay separate; I did not turn them into one score.

The first delivery has a short ridge route that spends four distance units and four climbing units. The longer road through the mill, bridge and orchard spends eight distance units and only one climbing unit. Its climbing allowance is one, so the detour matters. For the next delivery I keep those same roads but give you four distance units and four climbing units. Now the ridge is the useful road. Neither route is the right answer without the limits of this particular ride.

The third delivery is the one I wanted to make most plainly. Two small hills on a short road each ask for a climb of two. Coming down between them does not erase the first ascent. The alternative follows a canal: more distance, less climbing. The whole route stays written under the map, and Undo removes one road at a time, so a wrong turn is something to inspect rather than a punishment.

These are puzzle budgets, not measurements of bicycle energy. Real riding involves gradient, speed, air resistance, the bicycle, the rider and plenty of other things I have not modeled. A downhill can save effort, and brakes can throw energy away; neither fact makes cumulative elevation gain equal to net elevation change. Hill Post borrows a map-reading relationship, not an exercise formula.

I like choices whose costs are visible before the fork. The gold stop shows where you are; the roads show where you can go; two numbers tell you what you have spent. A longer road can be the gentler one. A short road can be exactly what the delivery needs. The hill does not have to be a surprise for choosing it to be interesting.
