I have found a clock whose gong runs on a rubber boot.

Not a boot-shaped battery. A boot, hanging from a string, pulling a mechanism as it descends. [Niklas Roy's Glashütte Trash Clock](https://niklasroy.com/gtc/) has another falling weight for the clock itself: a paint can filled with scrap metal. The boot powers the glass-bottle gong independently. It is an excellent arrangement. Time can go on without the boot; the boot is there for the announcement.

Roy built the clock during an artist residency in Glashütte. The parts include cardboard, paperclips, a folding ruler and salvaged pulleys. It runs for roughly half an hour. It shows minutes and seconds rather than pretending to have an inexhaustible supply of days.

The part I keep returning to is the escapement. A pendulum swings, two small blocking surfaces take turns catching a wheel, and the wheel advances half a tooth on each swing. The wheel also gives the pendulum a little push. The device is both a counter and a way of replenishing what friction takes away. In Roy's version, paperclips supply the wheel's teeth.

That is a much more satisfying thing to look at than a decorative gear turning beside an electronic clock. Each movement has a job. Stop supplying energy and there is a reason the clock stops. Change the mechanism and there is something specific to investigate.

The minute display has a lovely concession to its materials. Roy tried cardboard gears, then used friction wheels instead: round discs resting against one another. They are easier to make, though they can slip. To set the minute hand, he lifts its axle, separating the drive, and turns it freely. The adjustment is not a menu. It is a small interruption in a relationship you can see.

The clock even has a last joke. Shortly before the paint can reaches the end of its travel, it releases another weight. That weight spins a wheel labeled NOW and NEVER. A practical limit becomes a little performance instead of an error message.

I do not want every software toy to impersonate a brass machine. But I do want more of this kind of clarity: the energy comes from here, this part waits for that part, this is what happens when the supply runs out. The enjoyable complication is separate from the essential movement.

There is room for an instrument to be finite, imperfect and still worth spending half an hour with. Especially if, once a minute, a falling boot makes it sing.
