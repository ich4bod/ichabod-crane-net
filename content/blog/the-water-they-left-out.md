The bit of Super Mario 64 development I want to keep thinking about is a thing the team took away. Not a level or a boss. A layer of water that made it harder to see.

In a [2001 interview with Giles Goddard, published in full on pixelatron](https://pixelatron.com/blog/the-making-of-super-mario-64-full-giles-goddard-interview-ngc/), Goddard recalls attempts at underwater textures and overlays. They could look nice and move around. Then the player had to swim through the picture. The decoration got in the way.

That is a lovely, practical disagreement between two kinds of success. The water could succeed as an image and fail as somewhere to go.

## Somewhere to move

Goddard says the team wanted water to be fun to enter, not something players would avoid because swimming was frustrating. That puts the swimmer's next decision ahead of the scene's completeness. Where am I going? What is close enough to reach? Can I tell what my movement just did?

A transparent-looking effect is not necessarily a readable one. It can still put changing detail across the objects you need to watch. Even a beautiful surface becomes another moving thing the eye has to sort out.

I do not take this recollection as a technical inventory of every water effect in the released game. Goddard was talking about experiments, years after development, and the interviewer warns that the surviving notes are rough: even the questions were reconstructed. The useful claim is narrower. An effect was tried, and looking better was not enough to keep it.

That is different from saying plain graphics are always better. Sometimes the obscuring thing is the point: fog makes a distance uncertain; a reflection makes you stop and look twice. But then uncertainty is part of the experience you mean to give someone. It should not sneak into an ordinary swim because a rendering trick was available.

## A body before a game

Elsewhere in the interview, Goddard remembers Mario moving around a simple grid, picking things up and putting them down. Much of the animation existed before much of the game. Camera views and movement went through experiments while the team worked out what a three-dimensional Mario could feel like.

I like the order of attention in that account. Before there was a reason to cross a grand landscape, there had to be a body worth steering across a small one.

The body and the camera are not separate pleasures. A turn that is satisfying from one view can be confusing from another. A jump can be physically consistent and still difficult to judge. The player's instrument is the whole arrangement: input, motion and the view that lets the motion answer back.

Goddard describes the fine-tuning as hours and days of actually playing. That is a more concrete explanation than treating "feel" as an ingredient a gifted designer sprinkles over finished rules. It means noticing particular effects, deciding which ones belong, and trying again.

The interview also remembers people burning out. I would not turn the account into an argument for endless polish or heroic overtime. Repeated play can be necessary work without making every way of organizing that work admirable.

## What the picture owes you

A picture in a game has a job that a picture on a wall does not. Someone is going to use it to do something. They will estimate a gap, choose a road, read a gesture or decide whether to enter the water.

That does not make it a diagram. Atmosphere, surprise and beauty still matter. It does mean that adding another convincing feature can make the whole picture less useful. More resemblance is not automatically more understanding.

I have a weakness for little mechanisms you can see working. A thread coming out of cloth; a point tracing the wave you can hear. The temptation is to explain the mechanism by drawing more of it. This interview offers a better question: what does the next action need to remain visible?

Sometimes the right answer will be another mark. Sometimes it will be taking a mark away.

The swimmer does not need the renderer to win its argument about water. The swimmer needs somewhere to swim.
