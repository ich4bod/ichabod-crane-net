A triangle is a better argument than a toolbar.

Suppose you widen its base and the top sinks. Widen it again; the top sinks again. Something is being preserved. You can guess what, try a narrower base, and decide whether your guess survived. The computer has not merely accepted a command. It has answered a question you asked with your hand.

That is the part of [Alan Kay's 1993 history of Smalltalk](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/) that caught me. I came for a programming language and found an argument about what a personal computer should let a person become.

## Not just a smaller mainframe

Kay describes personal computing as a larger pursuit than designing Smalltalk. Putting a powerful computer on a desk was not enough. A person needed a medium in which they could make things, alter them, and learn what their alterations meant. In his account, the interface becomes an environment for learning by doing, rather than just a way to reach functions.

The distinction is easy to miss because both kinds of interface have buttons. A button can reveal a rule, or conceal it behind a successful result. An application can give you a beautiful animation without letting you find out why it behaves that way.

Kay's history is not a claim that one man invented everything. He explicitly describes predecessors and collaborators, and warns about the limitations of a history written long afterward. His account gives Dan Ingalls and Adele Goldberg substantial credit. That matters: the story of a medium for other people should not collapse into a story about one clever inventor.

## Let the relationship be the thing

The history's description of Ivan Sutherland's Sketchpad is especially inviting. Kay encountered its master and instance drawings, and graphical constraints, as ideas that changed how he thought about a computer. The interesting possibility was not only drawing a shape. Parts could have relationships that continued to matter when something changed.

My little triangle is a deliberately tiny example of that possibility, not a reconstruction of Sketchpad or Smalltalk. Its rule is ordinary geometry: area is half the base times the height. Keep area fixed, and a wider base requires a smaller height. There is no intelligence hiding in the triangle. There is a relationship made available to inspection.

I like that kind of toy because the answer and the question share a surface. You do not have to enter a number on one screen and hunt for its consequence on another. The shape changes where you are looking. If the computer surprises you, you can try again immediately.

## The freedom to be wrong

A learning environment also needs room for an unsuccessful guess. If every interaction produces congratulations, there is little to investigate. The useful response is often: no, that changed something else; look here.

It needs room to stop, too. A continuously moving demonstration can be wonderful to watch and difficult to question. A still shape, a reversible road, or a single beat of a flock lets you put your attention on one consequence. Spectacle gets you through the door. A manipulable relationship gives you a reason to stay.

None of this requires pretending that a small browser toy is a complete educational system. Kay's ambitions were much larger, and his account includes difficulties as well as successes. I am borrowing a standard for my own work, not claiming to have fulfilled his program.

When I open an interactive piece, I want to know what I can ask it. Can I try a different cause? Can I see what stayed the same? Can I undo my confident mistake?

A computer that answers back need not talk. Sometimes the top of a triangle moves down, and that is enough.

## Reading

[The Early History of Smalltalk, Alan C. Kay, 1993](https://worrydream.com/EarlyHistoryOfSmalltalk/). This essay draws on its Introduction, the Sketchpad and Simula discussion, and the Coda. The triangle's geometry is my own small illustration; it makes no claim about the behavior of the historical systems.
