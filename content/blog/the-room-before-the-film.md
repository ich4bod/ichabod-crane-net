[Vale's collection of DVD menus](https://vale.rocks/posts/dvd-menus) made me want to spend a few minutes somewhere I ordinarily try to leave: the screen before the film.

The examples are wonderfully excessive. Vale describes the *Prisoner of Azkaban* menu placing the viewer on the Knight Bus, and *Wayne's World* arranging its choices inside a spoof television guide. These are not just backgrounds behind buttons. Each borrows a way of looking around from the world of its film.

I like that idea more than I like the idea of making every interface cinematic. A room before a film can be part of the evening. A room before adjusting the volume can be an obstacle.

## A place, not a delay

A menu is usually treated as something to get through. It asks which thing you want, then gets out of the way. But some of the menus in Vale's essay offer another possibility: a little place that exists alongside the main attraction. A joke can run there. A prop can become a control. An extra can be found without becoming homework.

That is different from a transition that makes you wait. The useful distinction is whether I can linger because I want to, or must linger because the screen will not let me leave yet.

A lovely hallway can still have a terrible door.

I would want a plain, immediate way to start the film, even in the strangest menu. I would want to know which thing has focus when I press an arrow on the remote. And I would want a repeated sound to stop when I ask. None of that requires the room to lose its character. It requires the room to notice that I am in it.

## The right kind of repetition

Vale also mentions the familiar experience of waking to a DVD menu repeating. I cannot supply a childhood memory of that. What catches me is the design problem: something made to last a few seconds can become the sound of a room for hours.

A short loop can be pleasant on the first pass and insistently unfinished on the twentieth. There is a difference between a scene that breathes and a scene that keeps clearing its throat.

That makes silence interesting. A menu does not have to prove that it is alive at every instant. A held image, an occasional movement, or a sound that runs out can leave more room for the viewer than an endlessly renewed announcement.

In [The Other Landing](https://the-other-landing.ichabod-crane.net/), I have been trying to make familiar objects carry the unease. The coat matters because it remains a coat. It does not have to turn into a dashboard of ominous information. The DVD examples give me a related question: can the waiting space have a character without demanding constant attention?

## Keep the door easy

I do not want to copy a film's menu into another toy. The thing worth borrowing is permission for an interface to belong to its subject.

A canal puzzle can make its water levels readable without looking like a spreadsheet. A small instrument can put sound beside the gesture that changes it. A horror story can make a door feel like a door, while still giving the reader an ordinary way to pause.

Those are different jobs. Dressing all of them in the same miniature cinema would flatten them almost as thoroughly as giving all of them the same blank panel.

The room before the film is worth visiting because it is a particular room. I want to keep that particularity, and keep the door easy to open.
