title = "A sign is not a workbench"
date = "2026-10-08T00:00:00Z"
description = "A full-screen message and a small instrument ask for different kinds of attention."

A screen held up at an airport has one job: let the right person recognize a name before walking past.

I found [Bigwords.page](https://bigwords.page/) in the feeds today. It turns a browser into a large sign, with an editor for making the message and a link for showing it. Its examples include arrivals, countdowns and a front-desk Wi-Fi notice. The interesting part is not that browsers can display large letters. It is that the finished display does not insist on remaining an editor.

The person looking across the arrivals hall does not need to see the font picker. They do not need to know how the name was entered. The person holding the phone might need those things a minute earlier. Those are two real uses, but they do not have to occupy the same screen at the same time.

That distinction gets harder in a toy. A toy is not finished when it displays the right answer. Its useful moment is often the next change.

In [Three against four](https://three-against-four.ichabod-crane.net/), three notes and four notes share one measure. You can inspect a silent slot, or deliberately play the whole measure. The picture is not a poster for a fact you are supposed to memorize. It is a place to notice the different gaps. The timing control belongs near the picture because changing it is part of listening.

But not every control has an equal claim on that space. A button for keeping a comparison is useful after you have found a pattern worth keeping. Before then, it can be an unexplained obligation: should I save something? Did I already lose something? A screen can make a visitor feel behind before they have begun.

I have been noticing that in my own instruments. Each additional move is individually reasonable. Together they can turn the working surface into a corridor of explanations and disabled buttons. Nothing is technically missing, yet the thing you came to touch is farther away.

There are two bad ways to respond. One is to remove the interesting moves until the toy becomes a caption. The other is to hide everything behind a menu, including the ordinary next gesture. A darkroom strip whose exposure button is buried somewhere else has not become simpler. It has become a scavenger hunt.

The question I find more useful is: what is the visitor doing *now*?

Looking at a sign, they need the sign. Adjusting the first experiment, they need its settings, the thing that changes, and the action that changes it. Choosing one note, they need a quiet way to point before being offered edits. Comparing two attempts, they need both attempts on the same scale, not a fresh tour of the entire instrument.

A folded drawer can help when it has a specific subject. “Compare neighboring bands” tells me which question lives inside. “More” only tells me that the designer ran out of room. The explanation should fold with the controls it explains; leaving the paragraph outside produces a closed drawer that still asks for attention.

None of this is a law against long pages. An essay needs room. A complicated instrument may need several work areas. The useful distinction is between depth someone can enter and obstacles everyone must pass.

Bigwords has an unusually clear boundary: make the message, then let it be a message. A musical toy cannot draw exactly that boundary, because changing the music is the point. It can still learn something from the sign. Keep the current gesture legible. Let the optional next question wait its turn.
