There is a peculiar kind of deadline that does not stop a game. It stops the next computer from having it.

[Video Games Chronicle reports](https://www.videogameschronicle.com/news/casual-game-store-legacy-games-is-shutting-down-purchases-wont-be-re-downloadable-despite-keep-forever-slogan/) that the Legacy Games store will close on December 31, 2026, with previous purchases remaining downloadable until February 28, 2027. According to the report, games installed before that second deadline will remain playable on the device where they are installed. Delete one, however, and the service will no longer supply it again.

The report also quotes the store's slogan: "yours to keep forever".

I keep returning to the gap between those two sentences. One describes a game you can keep playing. The other sounds like a game you can keep having.

## A game has more than one doorway

A purchase, a download, an installation and a running game are not the same event. We pass through them quickly enough that the distinctions often disappear.

The first time, the sequence seems ordinary. Choose a game; acquire it; put it on the machine; open it. Once its icon is there, the machinery behind the sequence can retreat from view. That is part of what makes a good launcher convenient.

The second installation asks the machinery to come back.

Perhaps you have a new laptop. Perhaps the old drive failed. Perhaps you removed a game to make room for another, expecting to return when the mood came back. None of those is an exotic request. They are ordinary things that happen between a person and a computer.

If the first installation continues working while the next installation becomes unavailable, the game has not vanished all at once. Its future has narrowed to an arrangement of files on a particular device.

That is a more specific loss than "the servers are shutting down". The servers can go away without ending today's play session. Today's play session can survive without guaranteeing tomorrow's replacement machine.

## The shelf is not the book

A library view is a very persuasive picture. Rows of covers look like a collection. A button that once said Install seems to belong to the cover as naturally as a spine belongs to a book.

But the cover, the right to play, the local files and the service that supplies another copy can live in different places. Keeping one does not necessarily keep the others.

I am not making a legal claim about the word ownership. I am asking a smaller practical question: what can the person do when the current installation is gone?

That question needs an answer in verbs. Can they download it again? Can they install from something they already have? Does that installation need a service that still answers? A reassuring noun cannot do all that work.

Nor would I assume that copying an installed directory answers those questions. I have not tested these games or their launcher, and the report does not establish that a copied folder is a working installer. An escape route should be demonstrated before it is offered as advice.

## Promise the next opening

What troubles me about "forever" is not that a company failed to guarantee an immortal computer. Nobody can promise that. It is that the word lets a very ordinary next opening remain unspecified.

A plainer promise would say which doorway is being kept open, and for how long. Already installed games remain playable. Downloads end on this date. A new installation after that date is not supplied. Those sentences may be disappointing, but they let a person understand the object they have.

I like games partly because they wait. A small puzzle can sit untouched for a year and still be exactly the thing I wanted when I return. The waiting is part of the pleasure; not every game needs to become a continuing relationship with a service.

A collection ought to be understandable during that quiet interval, too.

The important test is not only whether the game opens today. It is whether the promise survives the entirely unremarkable act of wanting to open it again.
