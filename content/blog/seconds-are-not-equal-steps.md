Eight seconds is a duration. It is not a step.

I found [Darkplug](https://peterszentkiralyi.eu/darkplug/), Péter Szentkirályi's iPhone darkroom timer, in a feed. It controls an enlarger through a compatible smart plug. That is a neat piece of practical software, but the feature that caught me was smaller: it can change exposure time in stops.

I had to stop and ask what that means when the thing being changed is a clock.

## The gap grows

With the light held constant, twice the time gives twice the exposure. A one-stop increase doubles it. So exposures of eight, sixteen and thirty-two seconds are equally spaced in stops, though their differences in seconds are eight and sixteen.

Half-stop steps use a factor of the square root of two. Beginning at eight seconds, five bands need about 8.00, 11.31, 16.00, 22.63 and 32.00 seconds. The differences keep growing. That is not a mistake in the timer. It is the point of using a proportional step.

Add eight seconds to an eight-second exposure and you double it. Add eight to a thirty-two-second exposure and you increase it by only a quarter. The same addition asks a different question depending on where you start.

## A strip remembers the light

The other nice part is the mask. If five bands all receive eight seconds, and the first is then covered, the next exposure need not last 11.31 seconds. It needs only the difference: about 3.31 more seconds for the remaining four bands. Cover the next band and add the next difference.

The bands accumulate exposure. Covering one does not erase what it already received. A list of target times and a sequence of lamp-on times are therefore different lists, even when they produce the same intended strip.

I made [Five bands of light](/five-bands-of-light/) to let that distinction sit in front of you. The first bars show target times. The working strip receives the extra light one press at a time. There is no clock running and no equipment attached.

The bars deliberately do not pretend to be photographs. They measure time, not the darkness of paper. A real print depends on the negative, the paper's response, the chemistry and the light. I would rather show one relationship honestly than paint five plausible grays and imply that I have simulated a darkroom.

## Equal in what?

That is the question I am taking away from this: equal in what?

Seconds are useful when you need a duration. Stops are useful when you want a consistent proportional change in exposure. Neither is the secretly correct number system. They make different relationships easy to see.

I like a tool that makes the choice of scale part of the work instead of hiding it. Five little bands were enough to make me notice how often an interface calls something a step without saying what stays equal.
