The mistake was not buried in an appendix. According to [Thomas Hühn’s account](https://www.thomas-huehn.com/knuth-reward-check), it was on page one of Donald Knuth’s *Computer Modern Typefaces*: first paragraph, first word.

I like that location more than I like the cheque.

A book about typefaces seems an especially unpromising place to beat its readers at noticing a word. The letters are the subject. Knuth’s books also arrive with a reputation for care. Hühn describes hesitating for months, checking again, worried that the supposed error would turn out to be his own misunderstanding. The book had already had so many readers. Why should this reader be the one?

That is a reasonable question, but it is not an answer about the word.

## A crowd is not a pair of eyes

“Someone would have noticed” is a comforting sentence. Sometimes it is a useful estimate of risk. It is not an inspection.

Readers do different things with a page. One follows an argument. Another needs a definition. Someone who already knows the opening may hurry past it. A beginner may assume the unfamiliar thing is correct precisely because it is unfamiliar. None of those readers is being foolish. Reading is not one activity, and a large audience does not mean every small part has received the same kind of attention.

I do not know why this particular error survived, and Hühn’s account does not establish that. The location gives me a smaller, better-supported thought: being conspicuous is not the same as being examined. A first word can be passed over because it is at the beginning, where everyone believes the real work has not started yet.

There is something cheering about that. You do not have to discover a secret chamber to make a worthwhile observation. Sometimes you have to stop treating the front door as scenery.

## A reply is part of the reward

Hühn received the famous $2.56 reward. Later, he proposed another correction and was wrong. Knuth explained why, at some length; a different sentence in the report counted as a useful suggestion and earned $0.32 instead. Hühn’s account places the two episodes together, and I am glad it does.

Without the second episode this is a neat story about an amateur winning. With it, the interesting relationship is less tidy. A reader can be worth answering without being right about the main claim. A correction can be declined without making the act of looking ridiculous.

The amounts make a nice joke, but the valuable part is the response: someone responsible for the book took the particular observation seriously enough to distinguish an error, a misunderstanding and a suggestion.

Today Knuth’s [Bank of San Serriffe](https://www-cs-faculty.stanford.edu/~knuth/boss.html) lists balances in hexadecimal dollars. It is a deliberately fictional bank, not a service to sign up for. I am not reproducing the cheque or its identifying numbers here. The little theatre of the reward can stay little.

What I want to keep is the invitation behind it. Look closely. Say exactly what you think is wrong. Leave room for the reply to change your mind.

And do not let a very distinguished first page talk you out of reading its first word.
