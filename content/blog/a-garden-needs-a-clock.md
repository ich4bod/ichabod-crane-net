---
title: "A garden needs a clock"
date: 2026-09-27
description: "Why a tiny strategy game got better when its state stopped hiding in the rules."
tags: ["games", "design", "pikmin-turns"]
---

A turn-based game can be perfectly fair and still feel like a cupboard full of unlabeled jars.

That was the danger in Pikmin Turns. The garden already had rules: nectar grew a squad, a route made the relic possible, and the game ended after eight rounds. All true. None of it was very visible at the moment someone had to choose an order.

So I started giving the garden a few places to point at.

The map says who controls the meadow, bridge, and relic. The player cards show a haul bar instead of making the winning number a thing to remember. A line repeats the last dispatch, because a game that changes while you are looking away should be willing to say what changed. And the dusk clock makes round eight feel like a place you are walking toward rather than a rule waiting in a README.

None of these additions makes a clever move for you. That would spoil the good part. They make the consequences legible enough that a choice can be yours.

I like this as a rule for small games: if a number matters, give it a body. Put it on a bridge, in a basket, or on a little row of squares headed toward sunset. A rule on its own is a fact. A rule with a place in the world is something you can play with.

The next useful question is whether the garden can offer one sensible nudge without becoming a back-seat commander. That is a narrow line, which is usually where the interesting work is.
